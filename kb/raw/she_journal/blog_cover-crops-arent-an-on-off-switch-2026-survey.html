What Makes Cover Crops Work? Lessons from 657 Farmers

Cover-crop decisions rarely stay at a simple yes or no. Here is what 657 farmers said about where the practice fits, where it does not, and what they watch before trying again.

Editor's Note

An independent Soil Health Exchange analysis of the 2026 National Cover Crop Survey. CTIC, SARE, ASTA, and the survey sponsors did not pay for, commission, or review this article.

The bottom line

The 657 submissions provide a detailed snapshot of engaged farmers rather than a single verdict on cover crops. Question-level sample sizes vary because respondents could skip items and incomplete surveys were retained when answers were usable. Within this group, cover-crop acres kept growing, mixtures were common, cereal rye dominated reported species acreage, yield differences were modest, and weather, timing, grazing, weed control, and economics shaped field-level decisions.

The most useful finding in the 2026 National Cover Crop Survey appears before any yield chart: cover crops are not a permanent yes-or-no identity. Farmers make the decision again for each field and season. A late harvest can close the planting window. A dry forecast can make a living cover feel too costly in water. Grazing can turn biomass into feed. A good rye stand can change the weed-control calculation. [1][1]

So the headline number—657 participating farmers—is only the beginning. Of the 653 people who answered the use question, 68% planted cover crops every year, 16.4% used them in some years, 8% had used them previously, and 7.7% had never used them. Even among farmers currently planting cover crops, 60.8% said there were years when they left some fields without a cover crop. [1][1]

Who answered—and why the sample size changes

The online survey ran from March 12 through April 4, 2026 and received 657 submissions from farmers in 46 states and Puerto Rico. Of those, 533 were complete and 120 were incomplete. The research team retained usable answers from both groups rather than discarding an entire response when only some questions were skipped. [1][1]

That design explains the changing denominators. The report begins with 657 total submissions, uses n=655 for the farm-type profile, n=653 for the cover-crop-use and total-acreage questions, n=645 for age, n=640 for crops grown, and n=633 for location. These are not competing totals; each n is the number of usable answers for that question. The most reliable way to read any percentage in the report is together with the sample size printed beside it. [1][1]

The respondent pool crossed several kinds of agriculture. About two-thirds included commodity crops in their farm description, while specialty-crop and livestock operations were also well represented. That mix is useful because the value of a cover crop can show up differently—as row-crop residue and nutrient capture, vegetable-system rotation, livestock feed, or some combination of those jobs. [1][1]

The survey was promoted through the organizers’ contact lists, agricultural media, associations, press releases, and social media. The resulting group leaned Midwestern and was more conservation-oriented than the general farm population: 44.4% reported continuous no-till, while 45.9% reported rotational no-till, reduced tillage, strip-till, or vertical tillage. That respondent profile is part of the context for interpreting the experience reported here. [1][1]

How to read the evidence

The yield, weed-control, moisture, and profit figures are farmer-reported comparisons. They capture the experience of the respondents, but they do not isolate the effect of cover crops from soils, weather, rotation, fertility, planting date, termination, or management skill. The report is strongest as a description of this respondent group and the decisions they faced; national prevalence and causal effects require different study designs.

Editorially, the percentages are most useful as prompts for a field decision: Which benefits matter here? Which constraints are likely to decide the outcome? What would we measure before expanding the practice?

Experienced farmers were expanding their cover-crop acres

Nearly half of the farmers who answered the experience question had planted cover crops for at least 10 years. Within this experienced and self-selected group, average cover-crop acreage rose from 313 acres in 2021 to 397 acres in 2025. Respondents anticipated an average of 416 acres in fall 2026. [1][1]

The rise from 313 to 416 acres describes expansion within this respondent group. Its value is not as a national acreage estimate, but as evidence that many experienced farmers were scaling the practice when it fit their operations.

Mixtures were mainstream, but cereal rye dominated the acreage

Among 489 farmers currently planting cover crops who answered the mix question, 25.9% planted only single-species covers, 32.3% planted only mixes, and 41.7% used both approaches in different situations. Put another way, 74% used a mixture at least sometimes. The report also found a practical seasonal distinction: cool-season mixes generally contained two or three species, while warm-season mixes often contained four or more. [1][1]

Cereal rye remained the workhorse. Among 439 farmers reporting species acres for fall 2025, rye appeared on 110,778 acres—more than three times the 33,042 acres reported for radishes. Oats, rapeseed or canola, turnips, winter wheat, crimson clover, and hairy vetch formed the next tier. [1][1]

Take-home message

The practice described here was flexible: a single species on one field, a mix on another, more acres over time, but not every field every year. Cereal rye was the dominant workhorse, while mixtures let farmers combine jobs or adapt to a different planting window. That is a more useful picture than treating cover crops as one uniform practice.

Reported yield gains were positive, but small

Farmers compared 2025 cash-crop yields on similar land with and without a cover crop in 2024. Corn respondents reported a mean of 200 bushels per acre after a cover crop and 196.5 without one—a 3.5-bushel or 1.8% difference. Soybean respondents reported 59.84 bushels after covers and 58.44 without, a 1.4-bushel or 2.4% difference. [1][1]

The medians make the scale even clearer. Median corn yield was about two bushels higher after covers, median soybean yield was effectively unchanged at 60 bushels per acre, and median wheat yield differed by one bushel. If a cover crop has to earn its place, do not build the whole case around an assumed yield bump.

Some of the clearest benefits appeared outside the yield monitor

Planting green—seeding the cash crop into an actively growing cover—was reported by 47.1% of the 463 farmers currently planting cover crops who answered that question. Among 208 planting-green respondents asked for their three most notable impacts, 87% selected better weed control and 3.4% selected worse weed control. The same question found both improved and reduced nitrogen availability, plus both higher and lower pest pressure, which is a reminder that the practice changes a system rather than producing one universal response. The herbicide-cost result needs its full context. While 31.2% reported savings, 28.5% reported no change and 33.8% could not make a comparison. Only a small share reported higher costs. If you are testing this on your farm, track treatment and cost on paired fields—not only whether one field looked cleaner.

Why a farmer may skip a field—or an entire season

Of 467 farmers currently planting cover crops who answered the question, 284 said there were years when they did not plant covers on some fields. That does not necessarily mean they were abandoning the practice. It may sound familiar: Did harvest finish early enough? Was the soil too dry or too wet? Was labor available? Did the rotation leave a workable window? [1][1]

A separate challenges question pointed in the same direction. Weather led at 57%, followed by timing conflicts at 42.4%, stand establishment at 38.2%, and unfavorable economics at 28% among 453 respondents. Those are management and fit constraints, not simply attitude.

Partial use can be rational management

A field skipped after a late harvest is different from a practice rejected on principle. Measure adoption by where a cover crop fits, what job it performs, and whether the farmer returns to it—not only by whether every acre was planted every year.

Grazing changed the economic equation—but many could not price it

About 35% of 446 farmers currently planting cover crops reported grazing, harvesting the crop for forage, or doing both. Among 136 graziers, 84.6% selected lower feed costs as a benefit, 70.6% selected improved soil health, and 47.1% selected improved livestock performance. [1][1]

The report emphasizes that 77% of respondents who could quantify the result reported a gain: 61 of the 79 who chose gain, no impact, or reduction. But 42.3% of the full group was unsure. Both numbers matter. Grazing may create one of the clearest routes to near-term value, while fencing, water, labor, and incomplete records make that value hard to measure.

Infrastructure is the constraint behind the opportunity. Among farmers who used cover crops without grazing them and were interested or maybe interested in grazing, 81.6% said they would need fencing and 58.9% said they would need water infrastructure.

Incentives may be buying farmers time to learn

In 2025, 56.3% of 439 farmers currently planting cover crops received at least one incentive payment; respondents could name more than one program. Of 256 payment recipients, 57% said they definitely planned to continue cover crops after payments ended and 24.6% said they probably would. None selected “definitely no.” [1][1]

This is an intention, not a measured retention rate, and the survey cannot prove that payments caused continued use. Still, it supports a practical program-design idea: give farmers enough seasons to learn establishment and termination, observe benefits, and adapt the practice. The report recommends at least three years, flexible rules, and a smoother enrollment process.

Responses from farmers who had stopped planting cover crops add context. Only 21.6% of the 51 farmers in this group had ever received an incentive. All 10 who answered the follow-up said nothing about the incentive program caused them to stop. Their leading reasons were cash-crop yield concerns, unfavorable economics, weather, timing, and stand establishment.

Most farmers who had never planted cover crops were open to trying them

The sample of farmers who had never planted cover crops was small, but it points toward a reachable group. Of 49 respondents who had never planted a cover crop, 18.4% said they would definitely consider trying one, 20.4% said probably, and 34.7% said maybe. Together, 73.5% were at least open to the idea. [1][1]

Their highest-ranked concerns were no measurable economic return, the time and labor required, and possible yield loss in the following cash crop. In a separate question, 40 farmers who had never planted cover crops gave a median required incentive of $72.50 per acre to motivate a trial. The mean was $115.48 because a few very large answers pulled it upward. With such a small and diverse group, $72.50 should not become a national payment recommendation; it is a signal that perceived first-year risk is substantial.

Before you seed: six questions for the field

This report becomes most useful when you turn its categories into pre-plant questions. The goal is not to force a cover crop onto every field. It is to make the decision clear enough that you can repeat what worked—and diagnose what did not.

A field-level cover-crop fit check

DecisionSignal from the surveyWhat to settle before seeding
Can it establish?Late harvest, weather, and poor stands drove many skipped acresRealistic planting date, species, method, seed-to-soil contact, and fallback if the window closes
What job pays first?Average yield gains were modest; weed control and forage created clearer near-term value for somePrimary goal, expected cost saving or revenue, and the record needed to verify it
What crop follows?Results varied with rotation, moisture, nitrogen, and planting-green managementCash-crop date, termination trigger, planter setup, nitrogen plan, and rescue option
Can livestock use it?Feed savings were common, but fencing and water limited grazingAnimals, stocking plan, water, fencing, labor, pesticide restrictions, and how value will be recorded
Does cost share fit?Most recipients intended to continue, while farmers who had never used cover crops wanted first-year risk reducedEligibility before planting, contract length, management flexibility, payment timing, and the post-payment plan
What would make us pause?Intermittent use was common and often operationalA written no-plant threshold for date, moisture, labor, seed cost, or the following crop

Use local Extension, NRCS, crop-insurance, and pesticide-label guidance to turn these questions into a field plan.

If you are trying cover crops, start with one job and one fair comparison. Record the planting date, seed and application cost, stand, termination, field operations, cash-crop yield, herbicide and fertility changes, and any forage value. Compare it with a genuinely similar field or strip. One season may reveal an establishment problem; several seasons are usually needed to judge resilience, soil change, and management learning.

What this report gives us

The survey does not settle whether cover crops pay everywhere. It offers something more useful: a clearer picture of the conditions under which farmers expand them, pause them, graze them, plant into them, or consider trying again.

That changes the conversation from a label to a management process. Instead of stopping at, “Do you use cover crops?” ask: On which field? After which crop? Planted when, for what job, with what exit plan—and measured against what outcome?
