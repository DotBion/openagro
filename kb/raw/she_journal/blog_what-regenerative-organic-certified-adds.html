Regenerative Organic Certified: What It Takes and What It Costs

Considering Regenerative Organic Certified for your farm? Work through its three pillars, certification levels, soil testing, fees, and the buyer commitments that make the plan workable.

SH
By Soil Health Exchange Team|Tuesday, August 11, 2026|12 min read|44 reads
Print / Save PDF
Start with the farm’s goals

Regenerative Organic Certified builds on an organic baseline and adds requirements for soil health, animal welfare, and farmer and worker fairness. Choose a level by comparing the work your farm needs to do with the market you plan to supply. Budget for implementation and testing as well as certification, and agree with the buyer on volume, price, and who pays for the additional work.

If your farm is already organic, Regenerative Organic Certified brings another set of decisions: which practices to change, how much of the operation to enroll, what to document, and who will buy the crop. Work through those questions together. A certification plan is easier to carry through when it fits both the farm and the market.

The three ROC pillars

The three pillars connect land management with the people and animals involved in production. A crop farm will prepare different evidence from a livestock operation. Use the current framework to identify the criteria that apply to your farm; ROA also uses the name Social Fairness for the farmer and worker pillar ⁠[1]⁠[5].

What the three pillars mean on a farm

PillarWork to review
Soil Health and Land ManagementRotations, cover, disturbance, biodiversity, fertility, and a soil-monitoring plan
Animal WelfareWhere livestock are involved: feed, pasture access, housing, handling, and health care
Farmer and Worker FairnessWorking conditions, pay, worker voice, safety, and fair relationships with buyers

Use these topics to begin a gap review against the applicable criteria for your chosen level [1].

Producer certification and company licensing

Producers apply with organic certificates, their Organic System Plan, maps, operation details, and supporting records. Only certified organic land is eligible for ROC acreage. If you are still working toward organic certification, start with Part 2 ⁠[8].

Brands making consumer-facing ROC claims follow ROA’s licensing process. The application connects product formulas and suppliers with organic certificates, a brand Regenerative Organic System Plan, and proposed labels. A farm’s certification and a brand’s license each cover their part of the chain ⁠[9].

What changes from Bronze to Silver to Gold

ROC Framework v4.1 defines Bronze as the beginning level and Gold as the highest, with Silver between them. Annual recertification audits apply at every level ⁠[1]⁠[5]. Each level changes both the share of the operation participating and the practices required. Criteria marked optional at one level can become required at a higher one, and the operation must meet all required practices for the level it claims ⁠[1].

Bronze is an audited certification level. Choose the appropriate level based on site conditions, production system, workforce and livestock obligations, participating acreage, market demand, costs, and the capacity of the downstream chain.

Make soil testing part of the plan

ROA’s Soil Sampling Guidelines call for laboratory tests in the first certification cycle and repeat lab and field testing every three years. Arrange the lab, sampling locations, methods, and field observations with the certifying body before the first audit ⁠[11].

Keep the sampling locations and methods consistent so the results help you track change. The guidelines use the tests to understand soil condition; certification decisions are not based on improvement in the test results. That distinction matters when setting goals with a buyer: an audited practice and a measured soil response are different pieces of evidence ⁠[11].

Keep the soil record connected to the work

When the next soil report arrives, you will want to know what changed between samples. FieldLog, built by the Soil Health Exchange team, keeps cover-crop planting, tillage, input applications, sampling, and observations on the field’s timeline. The phone screens below use a sample season.

In Log work → Soil sample, select the field and date, then use the notes for sampling locations, depth, method, bag identifiers, and recent management. Add a photo when it helps you return to the same location. Keep the laboratory report under the matching field and sample identifiers in your ROC records. Follow the sampling plan agreed with your certifying body ⁠[11].

Before an audit, use FieldLog’s Pro full activity log to gather the recorded field work for the requested period. The organic certification guide explains the export. Complete the ROC file with the laboratory reports and the animal-welfare and worker-fairness evidence that applies to your operation ⁠[1]⁠[11].

Build the buyer agreement alongside the farm plan

Ask the buyer which crop, volume, and ROC level it needs, then put the price and purchasing period in writing. The governing documents define the certification requirements; prices and premiums are negotiated with buyers. Use that agreement to compare likely income with the work and costs your farm will take on ⁠[1]⁠[2].

Questions to settle with a buyer

SHE planning questions for discussing the investment with your buyer.

The Framework’s buyer criteria address fair contracts, sourcing plans, pricing, payment, transparency, and capacity building. Use those expectations to have a concrete conversation about how the relationship will work for your farm ⁠[1].

Budget for the work as well as the fees

  • Farm changes: identify the practices, equipment, workforce measures, and animal-welfare work needed for the intended level.
  • Annual work: include the Regenerative Organic System Plan, records, soil testing, audits, and renewal.
  • Product handling: price the storage, segregation, processing, traceability, and label work. Gold claims have additional processor social-fairness requirements ⁠[2].

For an individual producer with fewer than five distinct parcels, the listed application and initial certification fees total $650. Add the certifying body’s charges, organic certification, soil tests, and any changes needed on the farm. ROA invoices use Net 30 terms; consult the fee schedule for late-payment and reinstatement provisions ⁠[4].

Ask which ROA-approved certifying bodies serve your region and production system, and request their audit and certification estimates. Those charges are additional to the ROA fees in the table ⁠[4].

Connect the certified farm to the finished product

2. Certified lotHarvest lot, quantity, crop, producer, certification level, sale, and shipping identityKeep the farm of origin and level attached to the lot
3. Supply-chain actorsDisclosed participants, applicable licenses, ownership and activity map, separation, transaction records, and origin documentsCheck that every relevant participant and activity is disclosed
4. ProcessorApproved product flow, incoming lots, batch records, formulas, processing loss, inventory, sanitation, and outgoing lotsReconcile incoming ingredients, processing losses, inventory, and outgoing batches
5. CPG brand or claim ownerAppropriate ROA license, Brand ROSP and product documentation, verified suppliers, approved claim, and approved artworkMatch the brand’s license and product records to the approved claim
6. Finished productComposition threshold, lowest applicable ROC level, organic status, final label review, SKU, and traceable production lotsMatch the printed claim to the formula and traceable production lot

A finished-product claim requires each connection from the certified farm through the approved product label [2][3].

The current Labeling Guidelines describe ROC as farm-level certification: product claims refer back to the certified status of the source farm. Brands must hold an approved license for ROC marks and product claims, in addition to any USDA organic handler obligations described in the certification guide. When different ROC levels are mixed, the claim defers to the lowest level or uses an unspecified level where permitted. Gold claims must identify Gold, and labels or marketing using ROC marks require prior approval ⁠[3].

Check the finished-product claim

For ordinary food products, ROA’s labeling guide distinguishes 100% ROC content, at least 95% for the ROC product claim and seal, and 70% to under 95% for a “made with” claim without the seal. Below 70%, identification is limited to qualifying ingredients on the information panel. Water and salt are excluded from these calculations. Wine and textiles have separate provisions, and every proposed use of the marks goes through ROA approval ⁠[3].

Keep the lot and its documents together

Choose a shared way to link the producer, harvest lot, quantity, level, transaction records, processor batches, and approved finished-product claim. Give each partner access to the records it needs while protecting confidential farm information. The following checklist can organize that work:

  1. Link the certified producer’s legal name, organic certificate, ROC certificate, current level, crop, participating acreage, and effective dates.
  2. Issue a stable lot identity with crop, harvest, quantity, tier, origin, and transaction documents.
  3. Map every supply-chain actor, activity, ownership transfer, facility, applicable license, and disclosure status.
  4. Connect processor receiving, batch, formula, loss, inventory, finished-lot, and shipment records.
  5. Connect the CPG SKU to its formulation, claimable percentage, lowest input level, approved claim, approved artwork, and license.
  6. Notify affected parties when a certificate, level, supplier, formula, facility, license, or label changes.
  7. Give the producer control over confidential farm data while allowing auditors and authorized partners to access the records needed for verification.

A practical ROC readiness review

  1. Confirm that the relevant production land and scope hold an accepted organic certification and that the certificate, addenda, OSP, and maps are current.
  2. Read the current ROC Framework and every applicable governing document rather than relying on a summary or old checklist.
  3. Map which soil-health, animal-welfare, and worker-fairness requirements apply to the operation and what evidence already exists.
  4. Identify gaps, responsible people, audit timing, corrective-action capacity, fees, and ongoing renewal work.
  5. For products, map every producer, ingredient, processor, handler, broker, contractor, and brand role under both organic and ROC requirements — Part 2 covers the USDA organic side of that map.
  6. Submit product and label claims through the required review and licensing process before marketing.

ROC by the numbers

ROA's 2024 annual report, published in January 2025, reported 301 certified farms and ranches, 61,000 smallholder farmers, 18.4 million certified acres, 223 licensed brands, 1,900 certified products on the market, and activity in 44 countries ⁠[7]. These are global program figures, not U.S.-only counts.

Use the farm and ranch directory and current certification documents to check a particular producer, level, and crop before contracting ⁠[10].

Decide what would make ROC worthwhile

Put the proposed level, farm changes, testing plan, full costs, and buyer terms on one page. Assign the next step to a person and a season. That gives you something concrete to discuss with your certifying body and buyer.

The right starting level is one your farm can sustain and your market will support. You can value the soil, animal-welfare, and worker-fairness goals in their own right while making a clear plan to pay for the work.
