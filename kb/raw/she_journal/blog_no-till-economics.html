How many bushels can no-till give up and still pay?

Three long-term trials, an Iowa corn budget and a planter retrofit example show how yield, field passes and equipment costs move no-till margins.

SH
By Soil Health Exchange Team|Tuesday, November 17, 2026|6 min read|16 reads
Print / Save PDF

The saving starts on the day a tractor stays in the shed. Take two paid tillage passes out of the schedule in the Iowa corn budget below and you save $20 an acre. At a net corn price of $4.50/bu, that covers about 4.4 bushels of yield loss per acre. If you’re already down to one cultivator pass, the same switch begins $0.75 an acre behind. The first question is which jobs disappear on your acres.

Run the no-till budget with your field numbers.

Start with the passes you can remove

This example compares custom-hired operations on the same Iowa corn acres. The original system chisels, field cultivates and plants. The no-till system uses a no-till planter and adds one separate spray pass. Iowa State’s 2026 survey averages supply the operation charges. The herbicide materials and grain price are planning assumptions. Iowa State 2026 Custom Rate Survey, pp. 2–3

If the only pass you drop is the $19.20 cultivator pass, the $2.60 planting premium and $17.35 for added spraying and herbicide leave the $0.75 gap. An existing burndown can change that. When both systems already make the same trip, remove the $9.35 custom spray charge from the added-cost column and price the actual change in chemistry. With the $8 herbicide premium still in place, the one-pass example now saves $8.60/ac at flat yield.

A higher corn price makes each lost bushel cost more

Use the corn price after the harvest, drying and hauling expenses that change with each additional bushel. In the two-pass example, the margin change is $20 plus the yield change times that net price. A 4-bushel loss leaves $2/ac at $4.50 corn and costs $2/ac at $5.50. The same planter and pass schedule can clear break-even at one price and miss it at another.

Long trials show where the dollars came from

Zhang and colleagues analyzed roughly three decades of field data at each of three sites. Their 2026 budgets valued the estimated yield difference at 2024 harvest prices and compared field operations, seed, fertilizer and pesticides. The Ohio trials rotated corn and soybeans. The Michigan experiment included wheat in the rotation. Its corn and soybean results appear here.

The largest cost change was in field operations. At Hoytville, Ohio, the corn budget saved $23.59/ac on operations and added $2.38/ac in pesticide expense. The estimated yield difference contributed $12.84/ac. Holding yield flat leaves $21.21/ac from those cost changes. At the Michigan site, the corn yield estimate was 15.6 bu/ac higher, worth $66.15/ac, on top of $34.89/ac in operation savings. Pesticide costs rose $9.86/ac. A first-year budget can price the passes it drops up front and use a range of yield outcomes. Zhang et al., Tables 3 and 4

The chisel comparison also matters. At the two Ohio sites, the same paper estimated larger corn gains when the baseline was moldboard plowing, about $81/ac at Hoytville and $133/ac at Wooster. The Ohio data window begins roughly two decades after the trials started. Set your first-season budget from your operation costs and test the yield line with your own strips. Zhang et al., Tables 1 and 9

Pugel and colleagues built budgets from interviews with 100 farmers already using reduced tillage, some with cover crops. Against the operations the farmers described before adoption, the estimated increase averaged $53/ac for corn and $45/ac for soybeans, converted from $132 and $111 per hectare. Expense savings accounted for roughly $24/ac in corn and $17/ac in soybeans. Crop sales explained the rest. These retrospective budgets capture a mix of changes on established farms. If you introduce cover crops at the same time, track cover seed, termination and forage separately so you can see which line moved. Pugel et al., 2026

The cash saving depends on who owns the iron

A custom invoice can disappear this season. With equipment you already own, start with fuel, wear and paid hours you can avoid. Keep the ownership bill in the comparison until a sale or a different replacement plan changes it. Cost out your own schedule from those lines. The Iowa custom rates already include operator and machinery costs. Iowa State custom rates, p. 1

Suppose your owned-equipment budget shows $20/ac in operating savings before a planter retrofit, and the retrofit adds $5,000 a year in equivalent ownership cost after accounting for its useful life, the cost of capital and resale value. At flat yield, it takes 250 acres a year to cover the bill. On 400 acres, the retrofit uses $12.50/ac of the saving, leaving $7.50/ac for yield risk. At $4.50 net corn, that is room for 1.7 bu/ac of loss. On 200 acres, the retrofit costs $25/ac and the change needs $5/ac from yield or another purchase you can drop. Size the investment using acres you expect to plant over its useful life. UW–Madison Extension on cash and economic budgets

Put the first comparison on a field where you can harvest adjacent strips with the same hybrid and measurement method. Keep track of planting dates, every pass and each load of grain. At the end of the season, you will know what came out of the schedule and how many bushels that saving bought.
