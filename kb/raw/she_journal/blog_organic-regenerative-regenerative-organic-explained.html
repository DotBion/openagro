Organic, Regenerative, and Regenerative Organic: What Each Term Means

What do organic, regenerative, and Regenerative Organic Certified mean for the way you farm? Compare the standards, the practices, and the evidence behind each term.

SH
By Soil Health Exchange Team|Tuesday, August 11, 2026|7 min read|40 reads
Print / Save PDF
Three terms, three starting points

USDA Organic is a federally regulated production and labeling standard. Regenerative agriculture describes approaches to improving land and farming systems; each program sets its own requirements. Regenerative Organic Certified builds on organic certification with additional soil-health, animal-welfare, and worker-fairness standards. The useful question is what each asks of your farm and how that work is verified.

A neighbor plants cover crops. A buyer asks for certified organic grain. Another wants Regenerative Organic Certified ingredients. The work can overlap, but each conversation asks something different of the farm. Understanding those differences helps you choose practices, plan the paperwork, and decide which market to pursue.

What USDA Organic means

USDA describes organic as a labeling term for food and other agricultural products produced according to federal organic standards. Those standards address crops, livestock, wild crops, and handling. Organic production is intended to integrate cultural, biological, and mechanical practices that cycle resources, promote ecological balance, and conserve biodiversity ⁠[1]⁠[2].

USDA sets the rules, and USDA-accredited certifiers review farms and businesses against them. Certification brings an Organic System Plan, inspections, and records that connect production to sales. You can check a certified operation in the Organic INTEGRITY Database. Part 2 walks through that process ⁠[1]⁠[3].

Organic certification applies across grains, vegetables, orchards, vineyards, pasture-based livestock, processed products, and wild crops. The records should follow the way you manage the land: a field, orchard block, paddock, tunnel, or harvest area. Agroforestry and silvopasture fit within the relevant crop, livestock, wild-crop, or handling requirements ⁠[1]⁠[8].

Organic farming includes soil-building practices and input restrictions. Crop producers must manage soil fertility and crop nutrients, use rotations appropriate to the system, and prevent erosion. Tillage is allowed within those requirements, and certain pest-control materials are permitted under specified conditions. Review the exact product and use with your certifier ⁠[4]⁠[14].

What regenerative agriculture means

Regenerative agriculture describes approaches intended to improve soil function, biodiversity, water management, and the wider farming system. Common practices include cover crops, more diverse rotations, reduced tillage, and planned grazing. Some programs verify practices; others track outcomes or combine both. Oklahoma State University Extension’s overview explains this range of definitions ⁠[11].

Ask a program which practices it requires, what it measures, who checks the work, and what you will need to document. A regenerative program can include farms that use inputs prohibited under organic rules. Organic farms can also use regenerative practices, whether or not they join a separate program ⁠[1]⁠[11].

How the overlap looks in a field

Consider two farms planting rye ahead of a cash crop. An organic grower might terminate it mechanically and use cultivation for weeds. Another grower might use an herbicide to terminate the rye within a regenerative program that permits it. Both have planted a cover crop; their input rules, records, and market claims differ. This example is a useful starting point for asking a program how its rules fit your rotation ⁠[4]⁠[11]⁠[14].

What Regenerative Organic Certified adds

Regenerative Organic Certified® (ROC) is a specific certification overseen by the Regenerative Organic Alliance (ROA). It starts with USDA Organic certification or an accepted equivalent and adds requirements under three pillars: Soil Health and Land Management, Animal Welfare, and Farmer and Worker Fairness ⁠[6].

Farms, ranches, vineyards, and grower groups apply for producer certification. Brands making ROC product claims follow a licensing pathway. Part 3 explains the Bronze, Silver, and Gold levels, soil testing, fees, and the records that connect a farm’s certification to a finished product ⁠[6]⁠[7].

Connect the label to what is happening in your soil

Certification checks an operation against a standard. Soil-health monitoring shows how a particular field is functioning and changing. NRCS recommends looking at physical, chemical, and biological indicators together: water infiltration, soil structure, organic matter, nutrients, and biological activity each tell part of the story ⁠[12].

Start with a field question, such as why water ponds after rain. Record a baseline, sample consistently, and compare results alongside weather and management records. ROC has a defined lab and field-testing process, covered in Part 3. For any pathway, choose measurements that help you make the next management decision ⁠[12]⁠[13].

Give the field a record you can return to

Whichever pathway you choose, the useful record starts with the work: when the rye went in, how it was terminated, which inputs were applied, and when the soil was sampled. Choose the next step for your farm

  • If your buyer needs USDA Organic: gather field histories, identify the crop or product, and talk with an accredited certifier. Use Part 2 to plan the application.
  • If you are considering a regenerative program: ask for its written requirements, verification process, costs, and purchasing terms.
  • If you are considering ROC: compare its additional requirements with your current organic system and the market you want to serve. Work through Part 3.

Start with the decision in front of you: the soil problem you want to solve, the buyer you want to supply, or the records you need to build. Then choose the practices and certification pathway that support that work.
