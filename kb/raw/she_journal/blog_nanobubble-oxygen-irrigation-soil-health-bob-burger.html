Nanobubble Oxygen Irrigation: What It Means for Roots, Soil, and Crops

Bob Burger explains why roots need oxygen, what nanobubble irrigation studies have found, and how growers can evaluate the technology with their own soil, water, crop, and farm budget.

Editor's Note

Bob Burger provides nanobubble consulting and sells ChucaoTech equipment through IngenuityWorx. Soil Health Exchange edited this contribution and checked the cited research.

By Bob BurgerEdited by Soil Health Exchange|Wednesday, October 14, 2026|6 min read|109 reads
Print / Save PDF

When soil stays saturated or becomes compacted, roots can struggle even where water and fertilizer are plentiful. Oxygen in the root zone deserves the same attention as those more visible inputs. Plant roots need it to respire, grow, and take up nutrients.

Nanobubble oxygen irrigation offers a way to deliver oxygen through the water a farm is already applying. Its agricultural value rests on a practical question: can better oxygen delivery improve root function and crop performance in the grower's own system?

Why root-zone oxygen matters

Plant roots use oxygen for respiration, which supplies energy for growth and active nutrient uptake. When water fills soil pores and drainage is slow, oxygen supply becomes restricted. Irrigation scheduling, drainage, and soil structure all help determine whether roots have the oxygen they need.

This is a long-standing agricultural concern. In 1935, A. R. C. Haas described avocado roots deteriorating in nutrient solutions and recovering when air was supplied. He connected that observation to irrigation and drainage in the field[1]. Nanobubbles bring a newer delivery method to an old root-zone problem.

Nanobubbles are tiny gas-filled bubbles in water, commonly around 100–200 nanometers in diameter. Oxygen-filled nanobubbles can enrich irrigation water with oxygen before it reaches the crop[2]. The useful measurement is how much oxygen reaches the root zone, at what time, and for how long.

What nanobubbles do in soil

Oxygen delivery can influence roots, soil chemistry, and the microorganisms around them. A 2026 laboratory study irrigated small columns of commercial potting soil daily for four weeks. Oxygen nanobubbles raised the measured dissolved-oxygen level to an average of 28.7 mg/L, compared with 8.6 mg/L in the deionized-water control[2].

The same experiment found changes in bacterial community composition and predicted metabolic pathways. Those predictions describe potential microbial functions; the experiment measured soil responses rather than crop yield. Its small potting-soil columns give a useful starting point for understanding the process[2].

For a grower, the next step is to connect oxygen delivery with observable outcomes: healthier roots, plant-available nutrients, crop growth, and harvest. Each link deserves measurement in the production system being considered.

Soil health and nutrient cycling

Healthy soil is a living system of minerals, organic matter, water, roots, fungi, bacteria, and other organisms. Oxygen supports aerobic organisms that decompose organic materials and participate in nutrient cycling.

That connection is especially relevant where compost, manure, cover crops, and crop residues contribute to fertility. Microbial processing helps release nutrients from organic materials. In a 2019 tomato study using soil incubation and soil-column experiments, oxygenation increased microbial biomass, activity, and diversity. Plant-available nitrogen and phosphorus were higher under nanobubble treatment than under conventional pump aeration and the untreated control[3].

These findings make nutrient supply worth tracking alongside root growth. Base any fertilizer adjustment on soil and plant measurements, crop demand, and the nutrient plan.

Good physical management remains central. Build soil structure, manage traffic and compaction, maintain drainage, and schedule irrigation to match the crop. Oxygen-enriched water fits into that system alongside the practices that let water and air move through the soil.

What crop studies have found

The most important question for a grower is whether the treatment improves the crop. Research gives several useful comparisons:

  • Tomatoes: In the 2019 organic-farming experiment, final tomato yield was 23% higher with nanobubble oxygenation and 17% higher with conventional pump aeration than in the untreated control. Both oxygenation treatments improved soil biological measurements[3].
  • Maize: A two-year field experiment in saline clay-loam soil near Beijing tested micro/nanobubble oxygenation through subsurface drip irrigation. The highest-yielding treatment supplied water at 20 mg/L dissolved oxygen throughout the growing period and yielded 11.66% more than the untreated control. At 30 mg/L throughout the season, yield fell 10.97% below the control in one year. Concentration and timing mattered[4].
  • Sugarcane: A two-year field experiment in Guangxi, China, found improved yield and soil fertility under micro/nanobubble-water irrigation. Bacterial composition and predicted functions changed, while bacterial diversity decreased. The response involved changes in the community rather than a simple increase in diversity[5].
  • Lettuce: A study of drip irrigation with oxygen-nanobubble-treated wastewater reported improved soil aeration, lettuce yield, and plant physiology. It provides a crop-specific example where irrigation-water quality and oxygen delivery were studied together[6].

Responses also vary across systems. In field and controlled-environment experiments with sand-based creeping bentgrass putting greens, nanobubble-oxygenated water did not improve measured plant-health characteristics. Soil oxygen increased in one field year but was unchanged in the other two[7].

Read these results with their crop, soil, water source, and irrigation method attached. They help identify promising comparisons for a farm trial.

Where it fits best

Nanobubble oxygen irrigation is worth evaluating where oxygen supply is a plausible production constraint. Drip-irrigated vegetables, intensively managed protected crops, and heavier soils with slow drainage offer useful questions to investigate. Frequent wetting, restricted rooting volume, and compacted layers can all affect root-zone aeration.

Start by walking the crop after irrigation. Look for persistent wet areas, uneven root development, and differences between healthy and struggling plants. Check irrigation uniformity and drainage. Where salinity or nutrient imbalance is involved, include those measurements so the oxygen question stays connected to the whole growing environment.

Make the farm comparison useful

The strongest case for adoption comes from a well-designed comparison on your own soil, with your own water and crop. Use comparable treated and untreated areas, preferably with replicated plots. Keep crop variety, planting date, irrigation volume, and fertilizer management consistent, and record weather and any management differences.

Measure dissolved oxygen in the source water and at delivery to the crop. Pair those readings with soil moisture, root observations, marketable yield, and crop quality. Choose the measurements before starting so the season produces a clear answer.

The investment decision also needs a budget. Obtain a quote covering equipment, installation, electricity, oxygen supply where required, maintenance, and monitoring. Compare those costs with the value of the measured harvest change and any demonstrated input savings. Equipment costs and payback require a farm-specific assessment.

Oxygen is easy to overlook because it is invisible, yet roots depend on it every day. Nanobubbles give growers another way to investigate that part of crop production. Pair the technology with sound irrigation management, good drainage, organic-matter building, and careful field measurement. Let the roots, harvest, and farm budget show where it earns a place.
