Agricultural Carbon Markets in 2026: What Remains, What They Pay, Whether It's Worth It

Agricultural carbon markets in 2026 are more mature, but they are still highly conditional. Farmer returns depend on acreage, baseline practices, verification costs, and contract terms, so the practical question is whether a carbon program creates meaningful net value for a specific farm.

By Dawood Atta, Ph.D. · University of FloridaUniversity|Saturday, July 4, 2026|Updated November 15, 2026|5 min read|64 reads
Print / Save PDF

What changed

Agricultural carbon markets in 2026 look very different from the early years of enthusiasm. Awareness is high; participation is not. In a Trust In Food (Farm Journal) survey of U.S. row-crop producers — cited in USDA’s 2023 assessment of agriculture in carbon markets — about 93% of producers were aware of carbon markets, but only around 3% were participating.⁠[1]⁠[2] Interest, clearly, has not translated into a consistent business case.

The market has also been through a shakeout. Nori, a Seattle marketplace that sold soil-carbon “Regenerative Tonnes,” shut down entirely in September 2024, its CEO citing a stagnant voluntary market.⁠[3]⁠[4] CIBO Technologies did not close but shifted toward enterprise emissions software, and in 2026 it was combined with Indigo Ag’s Source business into a new company, Terion; Indigo’s farmer carbon program continues on its own.⁠[5]⁠[6] Across the whole voluntary market, transaction value fell roughly 29% in 2024.⁠[7] The sector is more cautious than it was at the peak of the hype.

What farmers can actually earn

The question most growers ask now is not whether carbon markets exist, but what they pay after costs. Headline prices can look appealing. Indigo Ag reports new credit sales of up to $80 per credit in 2025, and in January 2026 Microsoft agreed to buy 2.85 million soil-carbon credits from Indigo over 12 years.⁠[8]⁠[9] That is the top of the market, not the going rate. Indigo says its farmers take home 75% of net revenue from each credit sold, paid on a five-year schedule, with up to 20% of credits held back in a buffer pool.⁠[9]

Across all agriculture credits, including livestock methane and land-management projects, the average price was about $8 per ton in 2024 (Ecosystem Marketplace).⁠[7] Fees and withholding cut into that before it reaches the farm: in a 2021 farmdoc daily example, a $15 credit with a 15% fee and 25% withholding pays the farmer about $9.50.⁠[10] At the field level, a 2021 Purdue survey found that about 80% of farmers offered carbon contracts were offered $20 per acre or less, and about half of those $10 or less — useful for practices you were going to adopt anyway, but rarely a reason on its own.⁠[11]

What a soil-carbon program actually pays

  • Gross credit price: ~$8/ton average across all agriculture credits (2024); up to $80 per credit for Indigo’s new sales in 2025⁠[7]⁠[9]
  • What farmers receive: 75% of net revenue in Indigo’s standard program; about $9.50 per credit in farmdoc’s 2021 example⁠[9]⁠[10]
  • Credits generated: most cropland offset projects generate 0.25–2 tons per acre per year⁠[1]
  • Typical deductions: ~15% program fee + ~25% buffer-pool withholding — a farmer often keeps only about 60% of the credits generated⁠[10]
  • Contract offers farmers reported: mostly $20 per acre or less (2021)⁠[11]

Sources: Ecosystem Marketplace 2025; Indigo Ag 2026; farmdoc daily 2021; Purdue 2021; USDA 2023. Ranges, not guarantees.

What matters most

For row-crop systems with enough acreage, carbon markets can still be worth exploring — but mainly when the farm already runs compatible practices and can absorb the documentation burden. The economics improve when carbon revenue stacks with other incentives. That is why the 45Z Clean Fuel Production Credit matters. Created by the Inflation Reduction Act and active since January 2025 (extended through 2029 by 2025 legislation), 45Z rewards low-carbon-intensity feedstocks — and climate-smart practices like cover crops and no-till can lower the carbon-intensity score of corn and soybeans headed for biofuels.⁠[13]⁠[14]

One important update for 2026: the pathway to credit those practices under 45Z is now open. USDA’s final rule on technical guidelines for regenerative biofuel feedstocks took effect July 29, 2026,⁠[14] and IRS Notice 2026-53, issued in September, added guidance on counting regenerative agricultural practices in the credit.⁠[17] Treasury’s final 45Z regulations are still pending, so documentation and verification requirements may still change.⁠[13]

The current reality

A more accurate way to think about carbon markets is as a conditional opportunity, not a universal solution. Some farms — especially those that can enroll with little added cost — will find the payments worthwhile. Many others will find the complexity outweighs the benefit. It is also worth remembering that the federal landscape shifted: USDA cancelled the $3.1 billion Partnerships for Climate-Smart Commodities program in April 2025 and replaced it with a narrower “Advancing Markets for Producers” effort, while the durable cost-share routes — EQIP and CSP — remain through NRCS.⁠[15]⁠[16] The market is more credible than it was during the hype cycle. It is still uneven, uncertain, and highly dependent on farm-specific conditions.

The expert’s view

Focus on net value, not headline promises. Before assuming a program will pay, ask whether it fits your acreage, your baseline practices, your tolerance for a 5- to 20-year contract, and your capacity to handle the data and verification.⁠[10]⁠[12] The market may be climbing out of its trough — but for most farms it is still not yet a dependable income stream.
