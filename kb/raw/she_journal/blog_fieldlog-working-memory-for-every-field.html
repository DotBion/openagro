FieldLog: A Working Memory for Every Field

FieldLog keeps a farm's tasks, inputs, soil samples, trials, and yields with the field they describe. Follow one season through the redesigned app, see what changed this fall, and help us test it.

SH
By Soil Health Exchange Team|Sunday, October 4, 2026|12 min read|24 reads
Print / Save PDF

Updated November 14, 2026, with new screens from the redesigned app, what has changed since October, and a call for testers at the end.

By the time a fall soil test comes back, the questions about a field are specific. When did the cereal rye go in, and at what rate? Were the cores pulled before or after the fall fertilizer? Which strips in the trial were the checks? The answers exist somewhere: in a pocket notebook, a text thread, a seed invoice, a spreadsheet on someone's laptop. They are rarely in one place, and rarely attached to the ground they describe.

FieldLog keeps them together. It holds a farm's tasks, field work, inputs, soil samples, observations, yields, and trial layouts with the field each one belongs to, so a season can be read back as one account. FieldLog is one of the platforms the Soil Health Exchange team is building. When the conversation turns to soil, with an adviser, a certifier, or your own crew, it can start from the record instead of from memory.

The screens below follow a sample 2026 season on five fields: corn and soybeans, cereal rye seeded behind the combine, a four-treatment cover crop strip trial, and a small field in organic transition. They show the season as it stands in mid-November, with harvest finished and the rye up.

What changed this fall

FieldLog was rebuilt in November around how a farmer uses a phone in the field. The record underneath is the same. What changed is how quickly you can reach it and add to it.

  • One menu. Today, Fields, Log, Work, and More sit at the bottom of every phone screen and in a side menu on a computer. The tools under them follow your role, what you manage, and the programs you report to. Everything else stays one tap away under More tools.
  • Work, to do and done. The plan and the finished work now share one screen. Tick a job when it is done, and undo the tick if you tapped the wrong one.
  • Longer voice notes. Tap the microphone and say what you did, for up to 60 seconds. The audio stays on the phone if the signal drops, and you read the transcript before it fills in the log.
  • Photos that keep their time and place. A photo attached to a log keeps when and where it was taken, even when it uploads hours later.
  • Units that fit the farm. Each season is set to US or metric units, with its country and time zone, so rates and areas are entered the way the farm measures them. Grazing and the US program worksheets still use US units.
  • Grazing and perennial crops. Grazing records herd moves and how long each pasture has rested. Log work now covers pruning, weeding, mulching, shade, and frost protection for tea, orchards, and other perennial crops.
  • Research beside the question. Ask an expert opens from any field, and it can look up measured field-trial results for your county before you send.

Start with the plan you already have

Most operations begin the season with a plan in a spreadsheet: jobs by month, field, crop, and product. FieldLog imports that CSV or Excel file, matches its columns to tasks, dates, fields, crops, and rates, and lets you correct any column it reads differently. Each row becomes a task that moves through not started, in progress, done, overdue, or skipped.

The Today view opens on what needs attention. On November 14, that means fall soil samples on North 40 four days late, the sprayer to winterize today, and a biomass clip on the trial block and the 2027 seed order due Friday. The forecast beside the list comes with a spray check. At 47°F, it advises spraying with care.

A field can start as a name and a crop. Drawing its boundary on satellite imagery, or importing one as GeoJSON or KML, adds acreage, a map, and local weather. Plot outlines follow the boundary, so a crew can find a plot and its records while standing in it.

Record work where it happens

In the field, a record has to fit on a phone and survive a weak signal. FieldLog's Log work sheet starts with one question: what did you do? Sprayed, fertilized, planted, harvested, tilled, scouted, or soil sample, with irrigation, cover crops, and other work under More. Choose the field and the day, then add a photo or a note. Or tap the microphone and say what you did: FieldLog turns up to 60 seconds of speech into the note and fills in the work and field it recognizes. You check the log before it saves. A log saved without signal waits on the phone and goes through, with its original date, when the connection returns.

Products, rates, and costs entered with the work become input records, so the cost of a pass reaches the field's totals without a second entry.

Other channels feed the same record. Each season has its own email address, so a forwarded note or photo lands in the work journal. WhatsApp messages routed through OpenClaw arrive the same way. Both wait for review, and on the Pro plan, messages that clearly match a field and task can post directly. A reply to a task notification becomes a comment on that task.

Read a field's season in one place

Every task, log, input, and yield attached to a field appears on its page, newest first. Creek Field's reads like a season summary. Soybeans were planted May 12. Waterhemp escapes along the creek edge were spot-sprayed June 26. Harvest on September 28 averaged 63.1 bushels per acre. On October 1, the field was grid-sampled at 2.5 acres per composite, 0–6 inches deep, with a four-day CO₂ burst run on six zones. Cereal rye went in the next day at 60 pounds per acre for $666, with a note on why it went in early. By November 9 the rye stood 3 to 4 inches tall, and on November 11 the lab results came back.

That context shapes how results are read. Soil tests compare well from year to year when depth, timing, and lab stay consistent, as our fall soil sampling guide explains. A CO₂ burst result means more when you know what was growing and what had just been applied. The timeline keeps those facts beside the number.

Run on-farm trials with replication built in

A side-by-side comparison becomes evidence when treatments are replicated and randomized. The Plot & trial designer lays out a randomized complete block or split-plot design on a field's plot grid. It also accepts a design pasted from a spreadsheet or R output, or assigned plot by plot.

The trial shown here, CC-2026-01, compares four treatments in four blocks: a no-cover check, cereal rye at 60 pounds per acre, oats at 64 pounds per acre that winterkill, and rye with hairy vetch. Treatments are shuffled within each block, and the randomization seed is saved with the design. Observations are logged to individual plots, and fall stand counts are already on all 16. Export plot data writes a CSV with each plot's trial ID, treatment, replication, and harvest records, ready for R or JMP.

Keep organic transition records in order

Land must go three years without prohibited substances before an organic crop is harvested from it (7 CFR 205.202). FieldLog counts that period from the field's last prohibited application. Pasture Field's was June 14, 2025, so its panel shows 17 of 36 months complete and an estimated eligibility date of June 14, 2028.

Products entered in Log work are checked by name against the USDA National List, and a possible prohibited match is flagged before the log saves. Confirming one on a transitioning field resets the clock. The organic inspection packet, under Reports & records, exports every input application for a chosen period, with its date, field, product, rate, supplier, and National List reading, oldest first, the way an application log is reviewed. Your certifier reviews the Organic System Plan and approves inputs; FieldLog keeps the records that review depends on. Our organic certification guide walks through the full process.

Close the season, then plan the next one

Season review turns the record into a year-end account. By mid-November, 30 of 36 activities are done, one is overdue, and one fungicide pass was skipped after scouting found leaf disease below threshold. Nine costed input records total $15,306. Creek Field's soybeans made 63.1 bushels per acre, West 80's corn 208, and North 40's corn 197. When the same ground has an earlier season in FieldLog, the review compares yields year over year.

The review also names gaps to close before spring. Here, three fields have no soil sample on record this season, and one spray is missing its cost. Your own notes on what worked and what to change are saved with the season. Plan 2027 from this season carries the fields, boundaries, and unfinished tasks into a new season.

Ask an expert with the record attached

When a decision needs a second opinion, Ask an expert sends the question to the Soil Health Exchange network of researchers, agronomists, and practitioners, who answer free of charge. It now opens from any field's page with that field, its crop, and its county already filled in, and you can type the question or say it. The field's recent activities, inputs, yields, and observations can travel with it, and you can preview exactly what is sent. The answer is published on the Knowledge Hub, and FieldLog lets you know when it arrives.

Below the question, What research says near you looks up measured results from field trials in your county through OpenAgData. Only the county, crop, and practice are sent. It is a limited public preview, so some counties return nothing yet. When it does find a result, you can add it to your question or save it to the field's notes.

The question from Creek Field asks when to terminate the rye ahead of 2027 soybeans. Our cover crop termination guide works through the same decision.

Where FieldLog is headed

FieldLog is built on one principle: ask the farmer only for the facts that create trust. Acres come from the boundary, weather from the date and location, and cost from the product, rate, and area. The app asks for what only the person in the field knows: which field or part of it, what went on, and how much.

The next stages build on that principle:

  • Records for part of a field. A pass on the west half or a spot spray along a waterway will be recorded as a mapped area instead of the whole field.
  • Drafts from a sentence, a voice note, or a photo. Speech already works this way in Log work. Next, FieldLog will propose the record from a typed sentence or a photo and ask only for the field and area. Anything it parsed stays a draft until someone accepts it.
  • Reports for everyone who shares the work. Season reports for farmers; client rosters, follow-ups, and signed recommendations for agronomists; protocols, bulk plot observations, and clean exports for researchers.
  • Connections the farmer switches on. Questions to Soil Health Exchange and research from OpenAgData are in the app today, and we are connecting FieldLog with the other platforms we are building. FarmDeck will let an input be followed from purchase to use. Guidance from Soil Health Exchange will come back as a next step on the field. TerraValue will draw on the record to assess a practice's costs and environmental outcomes. Farmers will be able to contribute field evidence to OpenAgData research.

The same record already reaches beyond row crops. FieldLog's Landscapes section keeps properties, management zones, tagged trees and shrubs, treatments, and follow-up visits that compare the same plant over time. Grazing does the same for herds and the pastures they move through.

Start with one field

Choose one field and this fall's work: the harvest date, the soil sample, and the cover crop seeding and its rate. When the lab results arrive, and again when spring decisions come around, that record will answer the questions memory cannot.

We are looking for testers

The redesigned app is new, and it improves fastest when people use it on real ground. We are looking for testers: farmers, graziers, agronomists, and on-farm researchers, in the United States or abroad, who will keep one field or one trial in FieldLog for a few weeks and tell us where it slowed them down.

Testing takes a free account and a phone. Sign up at fieldlog.info, log this fall's work as it happens, and send what you find through Help and feedback in the app. We read every note, and testers' reports decide what gets fixed first.
