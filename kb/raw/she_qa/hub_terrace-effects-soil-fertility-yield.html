Why did our best dryland field yield worse after we built terraces?

22 readers

Field answerHayes County, NebraskaPublished October 1, 2023Updated Nov 15, 20263 min read

Key finding

  • The likely causes are topsoil removed or buried during construction, compaction from the earthmoving equipment and uneven water across the terraces. Whole-field fertility programs miss this because the damage sits in specific spots, so sample the cut areas separately and target manure, zinc and phosphorus to where the subsoil is exposed.

The challenge

“"I farm with my dad in southwest Nebraska in Hayes County. We farm roughly 3,000 acres, all of which is dryland. We produce wheat, corn and grain sorghum. We have one field in particular that has us stumped. The field is 99 acres. Its soil type is Blackwood silt loam. We had terraces built on this field in 2010. Prior to the terraces being built, it was consistently one of our best-yielding fields. Since the terraces were built, it has become one of our worst-yielding fields. This field was also ripped shortly after the terraces were built to try to prevent any compaction issues that may have resulted from the construction of the terraces. None of the fertility programs we have tried seem to remedy the problem. Any insight into the causation of this problem, or any solutions you might be aware of would be greatly appreciated."”

What terrace construction does to a field

Level terraces hold runoff in a flat channel behind a ridge, a design suited to semi-arid areas such as southwest Nebraska with slopes up to about 4%. Building them moves a lot of soil. University of Missouri and NRCS guidance notes that topsoil is often removed during terrace construction and that areas of exposed subsoil reduce crop production. Blackwood soils formed in loess and have a neutral surface over moderately alkaline subsoil, with free carbonates (lime) starting 20 to 36 inches down. Where scrapers cut below the topsoil, the new surface is lower in organic matter, and in the deepest cuts it may be limy.

Heavy equipment also compacts soil, restricting roots and slowing infiltration. Level channels can pond water in wet spells while ridges and cut slopes stay dry, and the fertile topsoil that was moved ends up spread unevenly, so some parts of the field gained nutrients and others lost them.

Why fertilizer programs haven't fixed it

A whole-field program averages good and poor ground. Colorado State Extension cautions that a composite sample from the entire field will not reflect conditions in eroded or leveled areas. Exposed subsoil is a common place for micronutrient shortfalls. University of Nebraska–Lincoln (UNL) guidance lists terraced or leveled land, removed topsoil and calcareous soil above pH 7.3 as conditions where zinc may be needed, and corn is sensitive to low zinc. A DTPA zinc test below 0.4 ppm is low, and 0.4 to 0.8 ppm is medium. Iron chlorosis is commonly associated with high-lime soils, and UNL rates grain sorghum as a crop likely to lose yield where it occurs.

Ripping after construction was a sound idea, but it works only under the right conditions. UNL's compaction guide says to rip when the soil is dry to tillage depth and to run the shank 1 to 2 inches below the compacted layer. Ripped soil can re-compact if it is worked wet or traffic is not controlled.

Map the problem, then target it

  • Overlay yield maps or drone or satellite imagery on the terrace layout and check whether low yields follow the cut areas, the channels or the ridges.
  • Sample cut, fill, channel and undisturbed areas separately for organic matter, pH, free lime, phosphorus and zinc.
  • Check for compaction with a penetrometer or bulk density samples, and dig to see where roots stop.
  • Put manure on the cut areas first. In an 18-year experiment on a newly built terrace, manure alone or with fertilizer raised soil quality more than fertilizer alone, and soil quality tracked crop yield. Colorado State also reports manure is quite effective at eliminating zinc deficiency.
  • Keep wheel traffic to set lanes, and stay with no-till or reduced tillage.
  • Ask NRCS or an agricultural engineer to check whether channels pond and water spreads as designed.

Expect a gradual recovery. On a severely eroded hilltop in western North Dakota, North Dakota State researchers needed repeated manure applications, about 10 tons per acre a year for up to three years and then about 20 tons per acre, before soil organic matter and available nitrate rose significantly. Cover crops can add organic matter and protect against erosion, but in a dryland wheat, corn and sorghum rotation they also use water, so try them on a few acres first.
