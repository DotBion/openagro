How much nitrogen credit do I get from cereal rye before corn?

388 readers

Field answerIAPublished April 26, 2026Updated Nov 15, 20263 min read

Key finding

  • Plan on little or no credit at planting. Rye can tie up nitrogen right after termination, especially once biomass reaches about 1,000 lb per acre, so terminate before it is 8 inches tall and consider about 30 lb of starter N near the row, particularly if most of your N goes on as sidedress. The 20 to 60 lb of N held in the rye comes back slowly through the season.

The challenge

“I planted cereal rye cover before corn. How much nitrogen credit do I get, when does it become available, and how should I adjust my fertilizer program?”

Verified expert

Rye biomass and termination timing set the risk

The answer depends on how much rye you grew and when you terminated it. With a lot of biomass, rye can tie up nitrogen, and depending on conditions you may see an N penalty almost immediately. If rye biomass reaches 1,000 lb per acre, consider starter nitrogen and/or sulfur applied near the row at planting. Terminating when rye is less than 8 inches tall reduces the risk of N immobilization and lost corn yield potential. Brassica mixes carry less risk of N tie-up than cereal rye.

Expect a small yield drag on average, with a lot of variation. Across five Iowa State research farms in 2009 to 2013, corn after rye yielded 5% less than corn without it, and the gap grew as rye biomass increased. In later Iowa State trials where rye was terminated at 6 to 8 inches, the gap narrowed to about 2%.

Where starter nitrogen fits

In Iowa State trials at four research farms in 2014 and 2015, 30 lb of starter N per acre placed 2 inches beside and 2 inches below the seed raised corn yield by 3 bu/ac on average, and by 7 to 14 bu/ac in three of six site-years. The researchers concluded that with early rye termination, starter N can offset the rye's negative yield effect in tilled or no-till systems, and that the higher 30 lb rate is especially useful if you plan to sidedress most of your N.

That work is still developing. When Iowa State added a third year, starter N on its own no longer significantly changed yield. Purdue University trials at three sites tested 0 to 75 lb of starter N after rye and found it raised early-season N uptake but had an inconsistent effect on yield. Iowa State also found the economic optimum N rate was nearly the same with and without rye, only 6 lb per acre apart, so the adjustment is mainly in timing and placement rather than total rate.

When the rye nitrogen comes back

Rye aboveground biomass often holds 20 to 60 lb of N per acre; Iowa State measured an average of 20 lb, ranging from 2 to 62. That nitrogen returns slowly. In Iowa State residue-bag studies, 60% of rye N following corn and 77% following soybean had left the residue 105 days after spring termination, so treat it as a season-long supply rather than a credit you can subtract at planting.

Disease pressure is another factor in the rye-to-corn transition. In Iowa field trials published in 2023, rye terminated 3 days before planting produced 2.1 times more biomass and 4.1% less corn yield than rye terminated 14 days before planting, and broadcast rye was linked to more seedling root rot and Pythium than drilled rye.

Before you terminate next spring, estimate rye biomass against the 1,000 lb mark and note its height. Leave a few strips without starter, and compare their yield with the starter strips at harvest to see what the starter is worth on your fields.
