When do cover crops and no-till start paying on an Iowa corn-soybean farm?

111 readers

Field answerIowaPublished April 16, 2026Updated Nov 15, 20263 min read

Key finding

  • Treat it as a front-loaded investment: costs run highest and benefits smallest in the first few years, and most measurable gains show up after three to five years. Tillage savings and cost-share can carry much of the early cost; in SARE's national budget at 2019 prices, cover crops plus a switch to no-till sat near breakeven in year one and turned positive by year three.

The challenge

“I'm considering cover crops and no-till on 800 acres of corn-soy. What will it cost me during the transition, when will I see returns, and what's the total economic case including reduced input costs?”

Assistant Professor of Soil Science · Texas A&M AgriLife Research · Stephenville, TX

What the transition costs on 800 acres

Expect added costs in the first few years for cover crop seed, which varies widely with species or mix; establishment, whether fuel and planting or aerial seeding; termination, typically herbicide in a no-till system; and equipment changes such as no-till planter setup. SARE's 2019 cover crop economics bulletin, built on a national farmer survey, used a median of $25 per acre for seed and $12 for hired seeding, or $37 per acre. Cereal seed can run as little as $5 to $10 an acre, complex legume mixes up to $50, and aerial seeding $12 to $18. At $37 an acre, 800 acres comes to about $29,600 a year. Spring termination adds $10 to $12 an acre if you are not already making a burndown pass.

Herbicide choice depends on the cover crop species and growth stage. In no-till, terminate the cover before it reaches reproductive stages, or it becomes hard to control and can interfere with planting the cash crop.

What comes back, and when

In the first one to three years, some producers see yield variability or slight reductions while management is still being worked out; this usually improves as the system settles. Iowa State trials show where that risk sits: corn following a rye cover crop yielded 5% less across five research farms in 2009 to 2013 and about 2% less in later trials with early termination, while soybean yields were unaffected by rye. Most measurable benefits arrive after three to five years, depending on soil, climate and consistency: better soil structure and water infiltration and retention, less erosion and runoff, gradual gains in nutrient cycling and fertilizer use efficiency, and more resilience to drought and heavy rain, which steadies yields.

Some offsets start in year one. If you now chisel in the fall and field-cultivate twice in spring, SARE valued dropping those passes at about $24 an acre, or roughly $19,000 a year on 800 acres. SARE noted that the basic NRCS EQIP payment for cover crops started at $50 an acre or more in most Corn Belt states in 2019; check current Iowa rates with your NRCS office. Iowa's Crop Insurance Discount Program takes $5 an acre off the next year's premium for fall-planted covers, for acres not enrolled in another state or NRCS cost-share program.

How the budget adds up over five years

SARE's corn budget, at $3.50 corn and 200 bushels, put the net return from cover crops alone at about minus $31 an acre in year one, plus $1 in year three and plus $18 in year five, before cost-share. Adding the no-till savings moved those figures to minus $7, plus $25 and plus $42. For soybeans with no-till, the figures were about $0, plus $24 and plus $34. Those budgets assume fertilizer savings that build to 30 lb of N per acre by year five. Iowa-specific numbers are tougher: Iowa State economists surveying Iowa farmers found average annual net returns to cover crops were consistently negative unless the covers were grazed or harvested for forage, and the farmers who came out ahead usually also received cost-share.

Over the long term, many producers find the combined system economically competitive with, or more profitable than, conventional systems, especially under variable weather. The way to know on your own ground is to keep your own budget. For each field, record seed, seeding and termination costs, the tillage passes you dropped, cost-share received and yields from strips with and without covers, and compare the totals each year from year one through year five.
