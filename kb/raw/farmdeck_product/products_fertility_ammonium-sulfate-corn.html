Sulfur for corn (ammonium sulfate)

Various suppliers (21-0-0-24S; also thiosulfate and gypsum) · Corn

FertilityConventional15–25 lb S/acGypsum for organic

What the research measured

The cited Iowa and Indiana studies reported sulfur responses in roughly half of tested corn fields, compared with about 5% of sites in the cited Illinois research. Soil properties, manure history, and trial design differ across these datasets; the percentages describe the studies, not all fields in each state.

About 150 Iowa trials, 55 Indiana field-scale trials and 40 (plus 81 historical) Illinois trials, two sets published in peer-reviewed journals in 2025,.5 independent sources.

What it is. Ammonium sulfate, ammonium thiosulfate and gypsum supply plant-available sulfate to corn, a nutrient that used to arrive free from the air in power-plant fallout and now largely does not: Midwest wet sulfate deposition fell 72% between 2000–02 and 2020–22.

Study conditions and limitations
  • Response concentrates on coarse, low-organic-matter, eroded or side-slope soils and fields without manure; Minnesota rates the probability high at 0–2% organic matter, moderate at 2–4%, low above 4%.
  • Rates: about 15 lb S/ac on fine-textured and 25 lb S/ac on coarse soils (Iowa State, Purdue); sandy soils usually need S every year.
  • Starter-only S at 5 lb or less paid in just 1 of 9 Indiana trials; the lowest sidedress rate tested (5–30 lb S/ac) captured the full response.
  • Soil sulfate tests and ear-leaf S were unreliable predictors in the Illinois trials; Purdue uses tissue S below 0.15% with an N:S ratio above 20:1 as the deficiency signal.
  • Elemental S reduced yield at 1 of 18 Illinois sites; sulfate sources are needed for current-season supply, and ammonium sulfate’s 21% N belongs in the N budget.
Read the sources · 5
  1. Corn response to sulfur fertilizer rate and source in Illinois ↗
    Peer-reviewedPreza Fontes, Jones, Greer, Schaefer, Kaiser & Fernández · Agronomy Journal 117 (open-access PDF at Illinois NREC) · 2025doi:10.1002/agj2.70169

    Forty Illinois trials (2009–11) found a significant yield increase from S at only 2 of 40 sites, where gains were large (+14.7% and +30.5%), while 34 kg S/ha reduced yield at two fine-textured sites. The 1977–79 Illinois survey found 5 of 81 trials responsive. Soil sulfate and ear-leaf S tests did not predict response.

  2. Long-term field study on corn response to sulfur fertilization in Iowa, USA ↗
    Peer-reviewedCrespo, Kovar, Hart, Roth, O’Brien & Ruis (USDA-ARS / Iowa State) · Field Crops Research · 2025doi:10.1016/j.fcr.2025.109990

    Twelve central-Iowa experiments comparing ammonium sulfate, thiosulfate and gypsum at 34 kg S/ha: only 17% of trials yielded more than the control even though S raised early-season plant S in half of them. Economic return averaged up to $27.6/ha at favorable prices.

  3. Corn Response To Sulfur Fertilizer In Indiana: Research Update ↗
    University trialCamberato, Nielsen, Salguero & Quinn · Purdue Extension, Pest & Crop newsletter · 2023

    In 55 field-scale strip trials (2017–22), sidedress S increased corn yield in 26 of 55; where it responded the gain ranged 3–34 bu/ac and averaged 11. Starter-only S had no effect in 8 of 9 trials, and a 2% organic matter loamy sand responded in 5 of 6 years, averaging 20 bu/ac.

  4. Crop Sulfur Fertilization This Spring ↗
    ExtensionSawyer · Iowa State University Extension, Integrated Crop Management · 2020

    Of about 150 Iowa corn trials, approximately 50% showed a statistically significant yield increase from S. Iowa research supports about 15 lb S/ac on fine-textured soils and 25 lb S/ac on coarse soils.

  5. Progress Report: Atmospheric Deposition ↗
    GovernmentUS EPA Clean Air Markets · US Environmental Protection Agency · 2024

    Wet sulfate deposition in the Midwest fell 72% between 2000–02 and 2020–22, from 5.3 to 1.5 kg S/ha per year: the reason free atmospheric sulfur no longer covers crop removal on many Corn Belt fields.

Before you buy, ask

  1. What did my soil test show, and did the cited studies respond at similar levels?
  2. What rate, source, and timing were tested, and what did the untreated check receive?
  3. How does the price per pound of nutrient compare with the sources I already use?
