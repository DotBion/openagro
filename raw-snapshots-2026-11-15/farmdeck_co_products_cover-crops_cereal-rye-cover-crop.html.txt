What the research measured
The cited long-term Iowa study measured roughly half as much nitrate loss through tile drains with rye. Following-crop yield responses differed by crop and management; the linked studies report corn yield reductions under some establishment and termination practices.
Two peer-reviewed meta-analyses, a 90,000-field satellite study, a four-site three-year Iowa N-rate trial, 19-year USDA-ARS drainage plots and a 70 site-year Iowa on-farm dataset.

What it is.
Winter rye drilled or broadcast after harvest to hold soil, take up leftover nitrate and suppress weeds until it is terminated ahead of the next crop. More than 85% of Iowa cover-crop acres.
Study conditions and limitations
In the Iowa State 2018–22 project, rye killed 3 days before planting made twice the biomass but cut corn yield 4.1% versus a 14-day kill.
Broadcast rye made 3.5 times the biomass of drilled rye in the same project but 7.2% less corn yield.
Planting green is low-risk for soybean, not corn: Iowa on-farm strips gained 3–11 bu/ac of soybean in 10 of 31 site-years, while corn lost an average 19 bu/ac in the 4 site-years that responded.
Rye residue releases under 10 lb N/ac in the four weeks after corn planting; Nebraska and Iowa State recommend 30–50 lb/ac of starter N, and those sources do not advise a reduction in the corn N rate.
The corn drag is worst on better soils, in cooler mid-seasons and dry springs; the nitrate benefit depends on spring growth and was absent where biomass stayed low.
Economics: Iowa State CARD estimates net returns of −$30 to −$69 per acre for rye before corn without grazing or cost-share.

Corn yield response to winter cover crops: an updated meta-analysis
 ↗
Meta-analysis
Marcillo & Miguez · 
Journal of Soil and Water Conservation 72(3), Iowa State University
 · 
2017
doi:
10.2489/jswc.72.3.226
Across 268 observations from 65 studies (1965–2015), grass covers such as rye neither raised nor lowered corn yield on average. Legume covers raised corn yield 30–33% only where fertilizer N was low or the system was no-till.
Recent cover crop adoption is associated with small maize and soybean yield losses in the United States
 ↗
Peer-reviewed
Deines, Guan, López, Zhou, White, Wang & Lobell · 
Global Change Biology
 · 
2023
doi:
10.1111/gcb.16489
Satellite yield maps for more than 90,000 Corn Belt fields in 2019–20: an average corn yield loss of 5.5% on fields cover-cropped three or more years and a 3.5% soybean loss, versus non-adopting fields. Corn losses were larger on better soils, in cooler mid-seasons and with low spring rainfall.
Corn Nitrogen Fertilization Requirement and Corn–Soybean Productivity with a Rye Cover Crop
 ↗
Peer-reviewed
Pantoja, Woli, Sawyer & Barker · 
Soil Science Society of America Journal 79(5), Iowa State University
 · 
2015
doi:
10.2136/sssaj2015.02.0084
In no-till corn–soybean plots at four Iowa sites (2009–11), corn yield at the economic optimum N rate was 6% lower after rye even though rye biomass peaked at only 1,280 kg/ha; the optimum N rate itself did not change. Soybean yield was unaffected.
Long-term conservation practices reduce nitrate leaching while maintaining yields in tile-drained Midwestern soils
 ↗
Peer-reviewed
Rogovska et al. (USDA-ARS) · 
Agricultural Water Management 288
 · 
2023
doi:
10.1016/j.agwat.2023.108481
Over 19 years (2002–20) of tile-drained corn–soybean plots in Iowa, an annual rye cover crop cut nitrate leaching 59% with minimal or no yield penalty.
National Farmer Survey Documents a Wide Range of Cover Crop Benefits as Acreage Continues to Expand
 ↗
Government
SARE / CTIC, 2019–20 National Cover Crop Survey · 
SARE (USDA-NIFA)
 ·

Among 1,172 farmer respondents, self-reported 2019 yields after cover crops were 2% higher for corn and 5% higher for soybean. The most common seed-cost bracket was $11–15/ac.
