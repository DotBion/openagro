Key finding
Weigh the legume's dry aboveground biomass at termination, multiply by its nitrogen concentration, and count about half of that nitrogen as the credit to subtract from your corn N rate. A lab test of the dried biomass gives a better nitrogen figure than species averages, especially when the stand is dense, mature or mixed with grasses.

The credit comes from biomass and its nitrogen content
A reasonable estimate needs two numbers: how much dry aboveground biomass the legume has at termination, and how much nitrogen that biomass contains. Legumes fix nitrogen from the air, so their tissue is nitrogen-rich, and the more biomass a stand produces, the larger its potential contribution to the following corn. Tissue concentration varies with species, growth stage and field conditions; species averages work for planning when a lab analysis is not available. For comparison, University of Nebraska researchers measured 3.7% N in legume cover biomass at Mead, Nebraska, against 1.9% in cereal rye.
Estimate the credit step by step
Just before termination, clip the legume from 5 to 10 representative spots in the field using a 1 m × 1 m quadrat.
Dry the samples, either air-drying for 4 to 5 days or oven-drying until the weight stops changing.
Convert the average quadrat dry weight to dry biomass per acre.
Estimate the biomass N concentration from species averages, or send a dried subsample to a commercial lab for total N.
Total N in biomass = dry biomass × N concentration.
Estimated N credit = total biomass N × 0.50.
Reduce the planned corn fertilizer N rate by the estimated credit.
Account for termination method and timing: N is released faster when residue is incorporated and more slowly when it stays on the surface in no-till.
Worked example.
 A hairy vetch stand producing 3 tons per acre of dry biomass is 6,000 lb of dry matter. At an average tissue concentration of 3.5% N, it holds 6,000 × 0.035 = 210 lb N per acre, and the credit is 210 × 0.50 = 105 lb N per acre. If the planned corn rate was 200 lb N per acre, the adjusted fertilizer rate is 200 − 105 = 95 lb N per acre.
The 50% factor matches other estimates. Oregon State's cover crop nitrogen guide reports that about half of the nitrogen in legume covers is released as plant-available N. In University of Wisconsin trials, frost-seeded red clover held 80 to 110 lb N per acre in its aboveground biomass, and a corn N-rate trial at Janesville in 2010 measured a 46 lb N per acre credit.
What moves the delivered credit up or down
Grass in the mix.
 Legume residue typically has a carbon-to-nitrogen ratio well below 20:1, where rapid release begins. Cereal rye and other grasses raise the ratio, and Nebraska researchers recommend terminating before it passes about 24:1. In a grass-legume stand, apply the legume concentration only to the legume share, or rely on a lab test.
Growth stage.
 Oregon State finds legume plant-available N peaks around bud stage; later growth adds biomass but tougher tissue.
Residue placement and weather.
 Surface residue in no-till decomposes more slowly than incorporated residue, so some legume N may not be available early in the season. Warm, moist soil speeds release; a cold, wet spring slows it.
Check the estimate in season and on the yield map
Soil Health Exchange editors suggest confirming the credit with Wisconsin's pre-sidedress soil nitrate test. Take 15 to 20 cores to 12 inches between rows when corn is 6 to 12 inches tall, about two weeks before a planned sidedress. University of Wisconsin Extension lists cover crop and forage legume fields among those where the test is most useful, and a result over 21 ppm nitrate-N means no additional N is needed. Where you can, run strips at the adjusted rate and the full rate and compare yields at harvest. Record species, biomass, lab N, termination date, weather, the nitrate test and yield for two or three seasons, and your own credit figure will become more reliable than any published table.
